These three images all use the **same subject (the postman)**, but each tells a completely different story through **art direction**. That's what makes them interesting from a designer's perspective.

---

# Image 1 — "No ringtone, just tring-tring of his cycle."

## Overall Essence

> **"A memory that arrives before the letter."**

The letter isn't the hero.
The postman isn't even the hero.

The **emotion of anticipation** is.

---

## Emotional Significance

Primary emotion:

* Nostalgia

Secondary emotions:

* Innocence
* Warmth
* Belonging
* Excitement
* Human connection

The image doesn't make you miss letters.

It makes you miss **being expected by someone.**

---

## Situation

A normal delivery.

Yet the illustration transforms it into a celebration.

The child isn't receiving paper.

She's receiving **someone she recognizes.**

---

## Character Design Psychology

### Postman

Expression:

* Wide genuine smile
* Looking upward instead of downward
* Open chest posture

Meaning:

> "I enjoy delivering happiness."

He isn't portrayed as an employee.

He's portrayed as a **bearer of moments.**

---

### Child

Hands raised.

Feet moving forward.

Body leaning.

Meaning:

Children don't wave like adults.

They throw their entire body into excitement.

The artist understands children's energy.

---

### Cat

Very important.

It adds:

* Domestic comfort
* Familiar neighborhood
* Everyday life

Without the cat,

the illustration becomes functional.

With the cat,

it becomes lived-in.

### 2. Performance Branding

### 3. Relational Branding

# The Flow of the Artist's Mind

If I imagine the progression behind these works, it feels like three different creative questions:

* **Image 1:** "What did a postman *mean* to people?" The answer is emotional memory, anticipation, and neighborhood connection.
* **Image 2:** "How do we make the same profession feel exciting to a younger audience?" The answer is to borrow the visual language of action films and games, turning reliability into heroism.
* **Image 3:** "How can this public service feel natural in today's messaging culture?" The answer is to transform the postman into a friendly mascot whose gestures become a visual vocabulary.

The subject never changes—a postman delivering mail—but the **creative intent** changes completely. One celebrates **human connection**, one celebrates **capability**, and one celebrates **accessibility**. That shift in intent is what drives every decision about pose, color, composition, expression, typography, and visual style.
